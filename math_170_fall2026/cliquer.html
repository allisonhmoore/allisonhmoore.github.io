MATH 170 · Graphs · Cliques, and a drawing that cannot cross

Cliquer cliques · planar drawing

A handful of dots and two people taking turns joining them up. Every line you draw is worth a point — and if that line finishes off a triangle, or a group of four all joined to each other, it is worth a great deal more. The line does not have to be straight: swing it round the long way if that is what it takes. The one rule about where you may draw is that your line may not cross a line that is already there.

How Cliquer is played

  1. Take turns. On your turn you draw one line between two dots that are not already joined. It may be straight or it may curve — whatever gets there.
  2. Your line may not cross a line that is already on the board, may not cross itself, and may not run through another dot. Meeting an existing line at a shared dot is fine — that is not a crossing.
  3. The game ends when nobody can draw anything. Highest score wins.
  4. You are paid for every clique your line completes — not just the biggest one. A clique is a set of dots with every pair of them joined.

Notice what is not on the price list: a 5-clique. It is not an oversight. A drawing with no crossings is a planar drawing, and K₅ — five dots with all ten pairs joined — is the smallest graph that cannot be drawn flat without a crossing. Curving the lines does not help: a curve is still a drawing in the plane. So a 4-clique is the biggest prize there is, and the rule that keeps your pencil honest is the same rule that caps the payout.

The number of moves never changes: whatever either of you does, the finished picture is a triangulation, and every triangulation of the same dots has the same number of edges. Once lines may curve, the outside of the drawing is a face like any other — you can always loop round it — so the count is 3n − 6 and the shape of the board drops out of it entirely. A whole seven-dot game is far too big to solve here, but the last few moves are not, and the computer solves them exactly. One honest difference between this and paper: a pencil line can be as thin as you like, and a drawn one cannot. In a crowded finish the screen sometimes cannot fit a legal curve in the gap it belongs in — so it draws it a little wide, crossing something, and marks it faded and broken. The move is legal; the crossing is the size of the board. Blow the picture up big enough and that curve would clear.

Try: play a board against "grabs the most now", then the same board against "plans ahead". The greedy computer takes every triangle the moment it appears; the planner sometimes leaves one sitting there, because taking it would hand you a four. Then try "seven in a row", where almost nothing can be joined with a straight line and every move is a curve.
